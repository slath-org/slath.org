Bots

A bot is an account for automation: a CI system, a release script, or an AI coding agent. It belongs to a person or an organization, acts only through the credentials its owner gives it, and is marked as a bot wherever it appears, so nobody mistakes its work for a person's.

For setting up agents with bots, see Working with AI agents.

Creating a bot

Anyone can create bots of their own under Settings → Bots. An organization's bots are created by the organization's owners on the organization's Bots page. Each bot has a page (/-/bots/<name>) for its tokens and SSH keys, which only its managers can open: its owner, or for an organization's bot, the organization's owners.

Through the API, with a token that has the user:write scope:

# A bot of your own
curl -X POST https://git.example.org/api/v1/user/bots \
  -H "Authorization: Bearer $TOKEN" -H "Content-Type: application/json" \
  -d '{"username": "alice-ci", "display_name": "Alice'\''s CI"}'

# A bot of the organization acme (organization owners)
curl -X POST https://git.example.org/api/v1/orgs/acme/bots \
  -H "Authorization: Bearer $TOKEN" -H "Content-Type: application/json" \
  -d '{"username": "acme-release"}'

A bot's name follows the same rules as usernames and shares their namespace: it can't be the name of a user, an organization, or another bot. GET /api/v1/user/bots, GET /api/v1/orgs/{org}/bots, and GET /api/v1/bots/{name} list and show the bots you manage. Other people's bots don't appear there.

A bot's page also changes its avatar (PNG, JPEG, or WebP, cropped to a square and stored without metadata) and its display name. Without an uploaded avatar, one is generated from the bot's name.

Tokens

A bot works through tokens that start with slath_bot_. They are like personal access tokens, with stricter rules:

  • Restricted to repositories. Each token names 1 to 50 repositories, and works for nothing else. Name only repositories the bot needs.
  • Limited scopes. repo:read, repo:write, issues:write, mrs:write, statuses:write, and user:read. Never user:write or admin: a bot can't change its own keys or tokens.
  • Short-lived. A bot token expires after 30 days unless its owner picks another period, and after 90 days at the most. Site administrators can change both (see Accounts).

The token is shown once, when it's created. Create one under Tokens on the bot's page; Slath asks for your password first if you haven't confirmed it recently. A site administrator can also create one on the server:

slath admin create-bot --owner alice --name alice-ci \
  --token-scopes repo:write,mrs:write,statuses:write --repo alice/app

slath admin create-bot creates the bot, and with --token-scopes also a token for the repositories given with --repo (repeat it for several); --token-days sets the validity and --output json prints the result as JSON. Tokens can't be created through the API, just as personal tokens can't.

GET /api/v1/bots/{name}/tokens lists a bot's tokens (never the secrets), and DELETE /api/v1/bots/{name}/tokens/{id} revokes one. A slath_bot_ token only ever authenticates a bot, and a slath_pat_ token only a person.

A bot still needs a role on each repository, like anyone else: add it as a collaborator or to a team. The token's repositories only narrow what that role allows.

SSH keys

For Git over SSH, give the bot its own key pair. Its owner adds the public key under SSH keys on the bot's page, or through the API with a token that has user:write:

curl -X POST https://git.example.org/api/v1/bots/alice-ci/keys \
  -H "Authorization: Bearer $TOKEN" -H "Content-Type: application/json" \
  -d "{\"title\": \"ci runner\", \"key\": \"$(cat ci.pub)\"}"

GET /api/v1/bots/{name}/keys lists them and DELETE /api/v1/bots/{name}/keys/{id} removes one.

Roles and their limits

A bot gets roles on repositories like anyone else: as a collaborator or through a team. A bot that belongs to a person can never do more than that person on the same repository: its role is the lower of its own and its owner's. A bot with maintain access to a repository where its owner only reads can only read; a repository its owner can't see doesn't exist for the bot either, in lists and search included. An organization's bot isn't limited this way, since organization owners manage it.

The owner of a bot also owns what's in the bot's namespace, such as forks the bot made; for an organization's bot, the organization's owners do.

When a site administrator suspends a person, their bots are suspended too, and they come back when the person is reactivated. A bot that was suspended on its own stays suspended, and a bot can't be reactivated while its owner is suspended.

Guardrails on protected branches

Branch protection (repository Settings → Branch protection, or POST /api/v1/repos/{ns}/{repo}/protections) keeps bots in check:

  • Approvals by bots don't count toward the approvals a protected branch needs, unless the rule has Count approvals by bots on (bots_count_as_approvers). Even then, a bot never approves its own owner's merge request, just as its statuses never count for its owner's required checks.
  • Bots don't merge into or push to protected branches, whatever their role, unless the rule lists them under Bots that may merge and push (bot_merge_allowlist). This covers merging by hand, merging when ready, updating a merge request's branch, and pushing directly. When several rules match a branch, every one of them must list the bot.

Sensitive paths

Some files steer what runs, who reviews, what the secret scanner allows, or what agents are told. Bots can't change them, on any branch:

  • CI workflows: .slath/workflows/, .github/workflows/, .gitea/workflows/, .forgejo/workflows/;
  • everything under .slath/, such as the reviewer file and the secret-scan allowlist .slath/secret-scan.toml;
  • code owners: CODEOWNERS, .github/CODEOWNERS, .gitea/CODEOWNERS, docs/CODEOWNERS;
  • agent instructions: AGENTS.md, AGENT.md, CLAUDE.md, and GEMINI.md in any folder, .github/copilot-instructions.md, .github/instructions/, .cursorrules, .cursor/rules/, .windsurfrules, .clinerules, and REVIEW.md.

A repository adds its own paths under Settings → Branch protection → Sensitive paths, one glob per line (deploy/**, Makefile; ** any path, * within a folder name, ? one character), or with PUT /api/v1/repos/{ns}/{repo}/sensitive-paths and {"paths": [...]}. Whoever manages branch protection can change them; anyone who can read the repository sees the list at GET …/sensitive-paths. The built-in paths always apply, and so do paths the instance's administrators add for every repository (listed next to the built-in ones).

Names match regardless of upper and lower case. A bot's push is rejected when a commit it adds creates, changes, renames, or deletes one of these files; the rejection names each file and commit:

remote: slath: bots can't change sensitive paths:
remote: slath:   .slath/workflows/ci.yml (commit 3f2a9c1d0e)
remote: slath: ask a person to make this change
remote: slath: push rejected

Commits the repository already has, such as a person's work merged into the bot's branch, don't count. Bots can't apply review suggestions to these files either. People's pushes aren't affected. Each rejection is recorded in the audit log.

On a protected branch, a merge request that changes a sensitive path needs an approval from a person with the maintain role (see reviews); bot approvals never count for it.

Iterating merge requests

A merge request a bot opens starts out iterating: the bot can push as often as it needs to without taking anyone's attention. Reviewers it asks for are recorded, but they aren't notified, and the merge request isn't in their review requests yet. Watchers, mentioned people, and assignees hear about it as usual, CI runs as usual, and an iterating merge request can't be merged.

When the work is ready, the bot marks it ready (PATCH /api/v1/repos/{ns}/{repo}/merge-requests/{n} with {"draft": false}), or a person who can edit the merge request does, with Mark as ready. On a protected branch it then waits until every required check passes on its latest revision; until then its attention in the API is waiting_for_checks. Its reviewers are asked once, when it's ready:

attention What it means
iterating The bot is still working; nobody is asked to review.
waiting_for_checks Marked ready; reviewers are asked once the required checks pass.
ready Reviewers were asked, as for anyone's merge request.

A failing check doesn't send it back to iterating, and later pushes don't either. To ask reviewers as soon as a bot's merge request is marked ready, turn off Ask reviewers of a bot's merge request only once the required checks pass in the branch protection rule (ready_waits_for_checks).

Rate limits

Bots make at most 2,000 API requests per hour (site setting [limits] bot_requests_per_hour), fewer than people. The response headers RateLimit-Limit, RateLimit-Remaining, and RateLimit-Reset say where a bot stands.

What your bots are doing

The bot lists (Settings → Bots and an organization's Bots page) show each bot's state, its open merge requests, its last push, when its next token expires, and its API requests this hour; a bot's page lists its newest open merge requests and pushes. In the API, GET /api/v1/bots/<name>/overview (user:read) shows a bot's managers and site administrators what it's up to: its state and whether a freeze applies, its open merge requests (the count and the newest 5), its newest 5 pushes, the token that expires next, and its API requests in the current hour. Merge requests and pushes in repositories you can't read aren't counted or listed. The request count comes from the server process that answers, so behind several Slath processes it's that process's share.

Stopping a bot

If a bot misbehaves or one of its credentials may have leaked, stop it with Stop bot at the bottom of its page, or through the API:

curl -X POST https://git.example.org/api/v1/bots/alice-ci/stop \
  -H "Authorization: Bearer $TOKEN"     # a token with user:write

A stop takes effect at once: the bot is suspended, every token of it is revoked, its SSH keys are removed, and a push it has already started is refused before it changes anything. Its memberships, merge requests, comments, and statuses stay. The bot's managers and site administrators can stop it; on a server, slath admin bot stop alice-ci does the same.

Re-enable bot on its page, POST /api/v1/bots/alice-ci/start, or slath admin bot start alice-ci re-enables a stopped bot. It comes back without credentials, so create new tokens and keys for it. A bot that a site administrator suspended, or that was suspended with its owner, can't be re-enabled this way. Stops and re-enables are in the audit log (bot.stop, bot.start).

Freezing all bots

To pause every bot of an organization at once, for example while you look into an incident, its owners freeze them with Freeze bots on the organization's Bots page, or through the API:

curl -X PUT https://git.example.org/api/v1/orgs/acme/bot-freeze \
  -H "Authorization: Bearer $TOKEN"     # an owner's token with user:write

While frozen, the organization's bots can't use their tokens or SSH keys and their pushes are refused, but nothing is revoked: DELETE on the same URL unfreezes them, and everything works as before. GET says whether they're frozen, since when, and by whom. Bots of individual people aren't covered by an organization's freeze. Freezes are audited (bot.freeze, bot.unfreeze).

How bots appear

A Bot badge follows a bot's name wherever Slath shows it: as the author of issues, merge requests, and comments, in reviews, review threads, the timeline, commit statuses, member and collaborator lists, and the audit log. A bot's profile says who owns it. Comments by bots are also marked as claims: Slath shows what a bot says, not that it's true.

Commits by bots

Commits a bot pushes show as the bot, with its avatar and badge: the bot is their committer, and their author too, unless the author address is a person's confirmed address (a bot that rebases someone's work keeps them as the author). Commits with the bot's no-reply address (<bot>@users.noreply.<host>) show as the bot wherever they're pushed from. The bot's page shows the address and the command that sets it for the agent or CI that commits as the bot:

git config user.email ci-bot@users.noreply.slath.example
jj config set --repo user.email ci-bot@users.noreply.slath.example

Bots have no other email addresses.

What a bot can't do

  • Sign in. A bot has no password, no session, no second factor, and no OpenID Connect link. The sign-in page refuses its name exactly as it refuses an unknown one.
  • Own organizations or create bots. Bots can be members of an organization but never its owners, and they can't create organizations or bots of their own.
  • Resolve a person's thread. A bot can't resolve or reopen a review thread a person started; it marks it addressed (PUT …/merge-requests/{n}/threads/{id}/addressed), and a person resolves it (see threads). A bot may resolve the threads bots started.
